RV Water Filters 2026: Don't Drink the Campground Water Without One

📖 9 min read Updated: May 2026

That first sip from the campground tap? Could be fine. Could taste like a swimming pool. Could have sediment, bacteria, or lead from old pipes. I used to skip filtering because "it looks clear." Then I replaced my water heater after two years of mineral buildup. Lesson learned the expensive way.

Three Types of RV Water Filtration

1. Inline Filters (Entry-Level)

Price: $15–30 | Lifespan: 3–6 months | What they remove: Sediment, chlorine, bad taste

These screw onto your hose between the spigot and your RV. Simple, cheap, and they handle the basics. Every RVer should have at least this.

The catch: They don't remove bacteria, viruses, or heavy metals. Fine for most campgrounds with treated water. Not enough if you're filling from a well or questionable source.

2. Under-Sink / Canister Systems (Mid-Tier)

Price: $80–200 | Lifespan: 6–12 months per cartridge | What they remove: Everything inline does, plus lead, cysts, VOCs

Installed under your RV sink, these use replaceable filter cartridges. Better filtration than inline, and you don't have to think about it once it's set up. Just change the cartridge every 6–12 months.

Camco TastePURE and Culligan RVF-10 are the two most popular. Both are solid.

3. Whole-RV Systems (Maximum Protection)

Price: $200–500 | Lifespan: Varies by stage | What they remove: Sediment, chlorine, lead, bacteria, cysts, chemicals

Multi-stage systems that filter all water entering your RV—not just the kitchen sink. Usually 2–3 stages: sediment filter → carbon block → (optional) UV purification.

This is what full-timers use. If you live in your RV and drink the water every day, this is the right call.

💡 The Combination Play

Run an inline filter on the hose for sediment and chlorine (protects your plumbing), plus an under-sink filter for drinking water. Two layers of protection for under $100 total.

The Water Test You Should Do

Before drinking from any new water source, test it. A $15 TDS (Total Dissolved Solids) meter tells you the mineral content. A $25 bacteria test kit tells you if it's biologically safe.

TDS below 50 ppm = great. 50–150 = fine. Over 300 = filter it heavily or don't drink it.

Zero ppm isn't necessarily better—your body needs some minerals. But high TDS means hard water that'll wreck your plumbing over time.

Maintenance Matters

  • Replace inline filters every 3–6 months, even if you haven't used them much. Bacteria grows in damp filters.
  • Sanitize your fresh water tank twice a year. 1/4 cup bleach per 15 gallons, fill, let sit 4 hours, drain, flush.
  • Never drink from your fresh water tank unless you've sanitized it recently and filtered the water going in.
  • Use a drinking-safe hose. Regular garden hoses leach chemicals. White or blue "drinking water safe" hoses are worth the $15.
